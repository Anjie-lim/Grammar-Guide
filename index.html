Module 1

Grammar Depends on Context

Learn how the task determines the kind of writing you should produce.

Good annotation writing starts with understanding what the task is asking you to write. A grammatically correct phrase can be appropriate in one task and inadequate in another.

Start with the task. Before checking grammar, identify the purpose of the field you are completing. Ask: What am I supposed to communicate here?

Match the writing to the annotation purpose

Annotation outputPurposeTypical structure
RationaleExplain or justify an annotation decision.Usually a complete sentence or sentences.
Image-generation promptDescribe a desired output or instruct a model.May be a complete sentence, imperative instruction, or descriptive phrase.
Action descriptionIdentify what a subject is doing.Often a phrase such as carrying a blue backpack.
Short labelIdentify an object, category, attribute, or class.Often a word or short phrase.

One scene, several valid outputs

Imagine an image showing a person carrying a blue backpack near a train station.

Rationale: “Image B was selected because the backpack straps are more clearly defined.”

Action description: “carrying a blue backpack”

Descriptive prompt: “A person carrying a blue backpack near a train station, natural daylight, realistic photography.”

Direct instruction: “Create an image of a person carrying a blue backpack near a train station.”

Notice that carrying a blue backpack is not a complete sentence, but it is appropriate when the task asks for an action description. The same phrase would not be sufficient as a rationale because a rationale needs to communicate the decision and its reason.

Do not overcorrect. A sentence does not become better simply because you make it longer. Correct grammar should serve the task, not replace it.

Starting with a verb is not automatically wrong

Imperative prompts commonly begin with a verb:

  • Create an image of a cyclist beside a lake.
  • Change the jacket color to black.
  • Replace the background with a city street.
  • Remove the reflection from the window.

These are grammatically appropriate instructions. Do not mark them as fragments merely because the subject you is implied.

Module 2

Writing Strong Annotation Rationales

Explain decisions clearly, completely, and with relevant evidence.

A rationale should make your decision understandable to another person without requiring them to guess what you meant.

A reliable rationale structure

DECISION + REASON + RELEVANT EVIDENCE State what you selected or decided, explain why, and point to the evidence that supports the decision.

Weak: “This one is better.”

Better: “Image B is better because the subject is clearer.”

Strong: “Image B was selected because the subject’s facial features are more clearly defined and consistent with the prompt.”

Check subject–verb agreement

The verb must agree with the subject. Pay particular attention when a sentence contains a phrase between the subject and verb.

“The image is clear.”

“The details are clear.”

“The quality of the details is high.”

“The features on the face are consistent.”

Keep pronouns clear

A reader should be able to tell what it, they, this, or that refers to.

“The first image was compared with the second, and it was clearer.”

“The first image was clearer than the second image.”

Keep the time frame consistent

Choose a tense that fits the task and maintain it when describing related events.

“The image was selected because the subject has clearer edges.”

“The image was selected because the subject had clearer edges.”

Watch for fragments, run-ons, and comma splices

A fragment does not express a complete thought when a complete sentence is required. A run-on joins independent clauses incorrectly. A comma splice joins two independent clauses with only a comma.

“Because the background is clearer.” Fragment

“The background is clearer the subject is centered.” Run-on

“The background is clearer, the subject is centered.” Comma splice

“The background is clearer. The subject is centered.”

Clear does not mean complicated

Use the simplest wording that communicates the evidence. Annotation rationales are not the place for unnecessary formality, repetition, or long explanations.

Module 3

Writing Prompts and Descriptive Phrases

Use the right prompt structure for generation, modification, replacement, removal, and description tasks.

Prompts can take several grammatical forms. The goal is not to force every prompt into a full sentence. The goal is to make the intended output or action unambiguous.

Common prompt structures

TypeExampleWhat matters
Descriptive“A cyclist beside a lake, overcast daylight, documentary photography.”Specific visual details and relationships.
Direct instruction“Create an image of a cyclist beside a lake.”Clear action or requested output.
Modification“Change the backpack color to green.”State exactly what should change.
Replacement“Replace the background with a city street.”Identify what is being replaced and its replacement.
Removal“Remove the person standing behind the subject.”Identify the target precisely.
Addition“Add a red umbrella beside the subject.”State what to add and where it belongs.

Imperative prompts are valid

Commands commonly begin with a base-form verb. In an imperative, the subject you is understood.

Create an image of a person reading by a window.

Change the shirt color to blue.

Remove the reflection from the mirror.

Add a small plant beside the chair.

Do not treat these as grammatical errors simply because they begin with verbs.

Build descriptive prompts in a useful order

A practical sequence is:

  1. Subject: who or what is shown.
  2. Action or position: what the subject is doing or where it is.
  3. Environment: where the scene occurs.
  4. Visual characteristics: lighting, style, materials, color, camera or realism cues when relevant.

“A woman holding a red umbrella on a rainy city street, soft daylight, realistic editorial photography.”

Use relationships that are easy to interpret

When several objects or people appear in a prompt, make their relationships explicit.

“A man beside a woman with a red bag.”

“A man standing beside a woman who is carrying a red bag.”

The second version makes it clearer who has the bag.

Module 4

Common Grammar and Word-Choice Errors

Catch high-frequency mistakes that can change meaning or make an annotation difficult to interpret.

Some errors appear repeatedly in annotation writing. A focused final check can catch many of them quickly.

Articles: a, an, and the

Use a before a consonant sound and an before a vowel sound. Use the when referring to a specific or already identified thing.

“a red umbrella”

“an orange jacket”

“the subject in the center of the image”

Apostrophes and contractions

FormMeaningExample
itspossessive“The image has its own background.”
it’sit is / it has“It’s clearer than the first image.”
yourpossessive“Check your annotation.”
you’reyou are“You’re ready to submit.”
theirpossessive“Their positions are consistent.”
thereplace/existence“The object is over there.”
they’rethey are“They’re standing near the door.”

Commonly confused words

WordsQuick distinction
than / thenthan for comparison; then for time or sequence.
loose / loseloose means not tight; lose means fail to keep or no longer have.
advice / adviseadvice is a noun; advise is a verb.
affect / effectaffect is commonly a verb; effect is commonly a noun.
everyday / every dayeveryday is an adjective; every day means each day.
accept / exceptaccept means receive or agree; except means excluding.

That and which

Use that for restrictive information that identifies which person or thing you mean. Use which for nonrestrictive information set off by commas.

“The image that shows the subject clearly was selected.”

“The second image, which was taken outdoors, was selected.”

Integrated example

“Image A are more realistic then Image B, its hands also look more accurate.”

“Image A is more realistic than Image B. Its hands also look more accurate.”

Several issues were fixed: subject–verb agreement (are → is), comparison word (then → than), and sentence boundaries.

Module 5

American English and Project Conventions

Apply the required English standard consistently without treating variation as error.

When a project specifies American English, use American English consistently. This is a project standard, not a claim that one variety of English is inherently better.

Common spelling differences

American EnglishBritish English
colorcolour
centercentre
realizerealise
theatertheatre
organizeorganise
travelingtravelling

Conventions go beyond spelling

Project consistency may also cover:

  • approved terminology and labels
  • capitalization of task-specific terms
  • formatting and notation
  • units and number conventions
  • terms or constructions the project instructs annotators to avoid
Use the project standard when one is provided. Do not replace correct language merely because you personally prefer a different wording. Make the smallest change needed to meet the stated standard.

Minimal correction

If an annotation is already grammatically correct and follows the project standard, leave it alone. If it contains a genuine error, correct the error without unnecessarily rewriting the rest.

Original: “The subject is centered because the composition is balanced.”

Action: Leave it as written.

Module 6

Write, Check, Refine

Use a short final workflow before submitting annotation content.

Grammar checking is most useful when it happens after you have written the annotation for its actual task. Use a quick self-check, then stop when the writing is correct and fit for purpose.

Write

Start with the task requirement. Decide what the output needs to communicate and choose the appropriate structure.

Check

Run through the important questions:

  • Did I answer the actual task?
  • Is the writing structure appropriate for the field?
  • Do the subject and verb agree?
  • Are pronouns and references clear?
  • Did I use the correct word?
  • Are sentence boundaries correct?
  • Does the wording follow the project’s English standard?

Refine

Fix genuine errors. Remove unnecessary words. Clarify ambiguous relationships. Do not rewrite correct language simply because another version sounds different.

Know when to stop. The goal is not to make every annotation sound formal. The goal is accurate, clear, task-appropriate writing.

A five-point final check

CheckQuestion
TaskDid I provide the type of output the task requires?
StructureIs this a sentence, phrase, label, or prompt in the form the task expects?
GrammarAre agreement, tense, articles, pronouns, and sentence boundaries correct?
Word choiceDid I choose the correct word and avoid ambiguity?
StandardDoes the annotation follow the project’s English and formatting conventions?
